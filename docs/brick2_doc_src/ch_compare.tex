\chapter{Comparing configurations, and the inlet angle}
\label{ch:compare}

Everything up to here certified one machine at a time. This chapter
asks the question a designer actually asks --- bell or plug, how long,
how much swirl, at what inlet angle --- and finds that the hard part
is not computing the answers but making them \emph{comparable}. It
also records the longest wrong turn in this program: four sessions
spent chasing a defect that lived in a measuring instrument rather
than in the flow.

\section{The question, and why it is mostly a bookkeeping question}

Thrust scales with mass flow. A bell computed in one posed world set
against a plug computed in another compares the \emph{worlds}, not
the nozzle types. So before any comparison is meaningful a common
basis has to be fixed and enforced:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item the same gas, the same chamber state $(P_0, T_0)$, the same
  ambient $p_a$;
\item the same \textbf{mass flow} --- which carries a bonus, because
  for a choked throat $\dot m = p_c A_t/c^{*}$, so equal mass flow at
  equal chamber state means equal \emph{throat area}, and therefore
  the thrust coefficient $C_F = F/(p_c A_t)$ and the specific impulse
  are directly comparable numbers with the size of the machine
  divided out;
\item the same \textbf{envelope}: every configuration is allowed the
  same maximum radius. A plug given unlimited radius wins trivially
  on expansion alone.
\end{itemize}

Each configuration is then optimized over its \emph{own} design
variable: the bell over its area ratio, the plugs over where the
spike is cut.

One subtlety makes the two sides the same physical quantity. For a
rocket fed from a closed chamber the axial thrust equals the
ambient-gauge momentum flux through \emph{any} surface that spans the
whole exhaust and encloses the hardware. For the bell that surface is
the exit plane. For the plug the same theorem applies at the march
start line: the flux there already contains the thrust of everything
upstream, because the free jet boundary between the lip and that cut
contributes exactly zero in the gauge ($p = p_a$ on it). So
$F_{\rm plug}(l) = F_{\rm in}(x_0) + \int_{x_0}^{l}(p_w - p_a)\,
2\pi y\,(-\mathrm{d}y)$ is the total thrust from the chamber through
the truncation --- the same object as the bell's.

\textbf{The bell wins by $0.282\%$, and that is resolved.} The
derived band on the thrust comes from refining the march itself:
$J = 1.161610, 1.161481, 1.161454 \times 10^{8}$~N at three

\section{Why they are so close: the thermodynamic ceiling}
\label{sec:ceiling}

The near-equality of the coefficients is not a coincidence and not a
disappointment. Both nozzles expand the same gas from the same
chamber to the same exit pressure, and energy conservation alone then
fixes the exit speed: $\tfrac12 q_e^2 = h_0 - h(p_a)$ gives
$q_e = 2742.136$~m/s with no geometry in the statement. Since the
basis forces the same mass flow, the ideal momentum thrust
$\dot m\,q_e = 1.16474\times10^{8}$~N is exactly the bell's measured
thrust --- an identity, because at its optimum the bell is perfectly
expanded (the pressure term vanishes) and its exit is exactly axial
(measured at $\theta_e = -10^{-16}$ in Chapter~\ref{ch:score}).

The bell sits \emph{on} the ceiling. Geometry can therefore only lose
against it, through divergence, non-uniformity and incomplete
expansion --- all effects of order one percent. That is the entire
playing field of contour optimization, and it is why this program
derives every tolerance instead of choosing one.

\section{A certification gap the comparison exposed}

Setting up the plug side required posing the flow the way a real
aerospike does it: arriving at the lip aimed \emph{inward} so the fan
turns it back toward axial, with the spike descending toward the
axis. Every certified axisymmetric plug world in this document poses
the opposite --- inflow axial, the fan turning it \emph{outward},
the ``spike'' rising away from the axis (Figure~\ref{fig:turndir}a).

These are not mirror images. The axisymmetric source term is
$\delta\,c^2 v/y$ and $y$ is always positive, so $\theta \to -\theta$
is not a symmetry: the outward case runs with $v > 0$ and $y$ growing,
so the source weakens along the march, while the inward case runs with
$v < 0$ and $y$ shrinking, so it strengthens. The planar oracle is
immune (no source, genuine mirror symmetry), so it is specifically
the \emph{axisymmetric} certification that only ever exercised one
side.

Outward turning was never a candidate design --- it was a test
fixture. An outward-turning plug leaves its whole exhaust at
$\theta_E > 0$ and collects only $\cos\theta_E$ of the momentum:
$10.6\%$ lost at the design turn of $26.7^{\circ}$, against nothing
for an inward design. It is the natural way to write a closed-form
corner-jet test field, and that is how it came to be the only case
tested.

\section{The radial-flow oracle}
\label{sec:radialoracle}

To find out whether that difference was a defect, the program needed
an exact axisymmetric solution with the flow running \emph{toward}
the axis. Spherical radial flow supplies one: every streamline is a
ray through a point on the axis, the speed depends only on the
distance from it, and continuity with the isentrope closes it as
$\rho(q)\,q\,r^2 = \mathrm{const}$. Putting the singular point
upstream gives a diverging flow ($v>0$); putting it downstream gives
a converging one ($v<0$). One formula, so the two cases differ in
nothing but the sign the equations see. And since the streamlines are
rays, a cone is an \emph{exact} wall.

\carrier{validation/a1\_source\_flow\_oracle.py}, \textbf{6/6 PASS.}
The free edge is excluded by construction: only points below a
rigorous dependence bound --- a straight line from the start line's
top at the steepest characteristic slope present in the field, which
nothing from above can outrun --- enter the comparison. Against the
closed form, the median errors are

\noindent The interior and prescribed-wall cells are clean for
inward-turning flow. A useful trap surfaced in building the negative
control: in radial flow \emph{every} cone is an exact streamline, so
perturbing the cone angle merely produces a different exact solution
and can never reject. The valid rejector curves the wall off the ray,
breaking the ray property, and separates by $29\times$. Even
rejectors must be chosen by physics --- the same lesson as
Appendix~\ref{app:f2rejector}, met again.

\section{The instrument, not the flow}

The mass non-closure that started this investigation is largely a
property of the meter. The conservation check integrated over the
march's \emph{last column}, which is a characteristic, not a plane,
and whose segment lengths are wildly uneven: on a descending spike
the longest is $0.84$~m against a mean of $0.064$~m. Trapezoidal
error scales as the square of the segment, so that one segment
dominated. Three measurements settle it: the free edge's own
streamline flux is $4\times10^{-11}$ --- machine zero, so it leaks
nothing; the wall leaks $0.016\%$; and the cells reproduce the exact
solution above. Per-column inspection finds the cause: only the
\emph{first} marched column has a coverage hole, $26\times$ the mean
row spacing when the wall rises and $39\times$ when it descends,
against $0.5$--$1.0\times$ for every other column --- because a
column is a characteristic while a vertical start line is not, so the
first column alone must bridge the two geometries.

The lesson is the one worth carrying: \textbf{when the quantity of
interest has a derived band available directly, derive it directly.}
The thrust's uncertainty was inferred from a conservation meter
measuring something else; refining the march and letting it measure
its own error --- the program's own first rule --- settled it in one
run.

\section{The inlet angle as a design variable}
\label{sec:inletangle}

By owner directive the initial turning angle is no longer posed: it
is part of the optimization. The RDE delivers its flow across a
\emph{straight} annular inlet between the spike start radius and the
cowl lip, and the inclination of that inlet is the design variable.

The lip fan turn $\Delta\nu$ is fixed by the expansion (from $M_i$
down to $p_a$) and does not depend on the inlet angle, so the exhaust
angle is simply $\theta_E = \theta_i + \Delta\nu$; the sweep is
reported in $\theta_E$ because that is what the physics cares about,
and the classic design condition is $\theta_E = 0$. The basis of
Section~1 is unchanged, with one addition: the axial mass flux
through an inclined inlet scales with $\cos\theta_i$, so the annulus
is re-sized at every candidate to hold the reference mass flow, and
thrust is compared at a \emph{fixed length} so that length is not
silently traded against angle.

\carrier{validation/a1\_inlet\_angle\_opt.py}. The blind sweep lands
at $\theta_E = 0$ --- the divergence prediction, recovered rather
than imposed --- and the measured penalty follows the $\cos\theta_E$
model in sign and order across the whole admissible window:

Two things are worth reading off. The measured penalty is
consistently \emph{smaller} than pure divergence, because the wall
pressure distribution partially compensates --- the trade the
closed-form model does not contain. And the curve is asymmetric:
turning inward past the optimum costs less than turning outward by
the same angle, which is the quantitative form of the directive's
premise that outward-turning aerospikes are not competitive. The
window is bounded on the inward side by geometry, not preference: at
$\theta_E = -8^{\circ}$ the spike descends to $y = 0.32$~m and the
march loses certification as the flow runs into the axis, where the
axisymmetric source diverges.
