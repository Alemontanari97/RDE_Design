\chapter{The referee, and Rao's multipliers vs the adjoint}
\label{ch:referee}

So far the program has only checked itself. This chapter brings in two
outside judges: a nozzle design code written by other people in
another language, whose whole flowfields we audit against our
equations, and the 1958 pencil-and-paper theory of the optimal nozzle,
whose predictions we measure on a shape our optimizer did not make.
Passing both means the engine solves the right problem, not merely a
self-consistent one.

\section{The referee comes back online}

\subsection{What you need to know first}

An \emph{ideal}, or \emph{minimum-length}, nozzle gives a perfectly
uniform, purely axial exit flow in the shortest length that can do it
(Chapter~\ref{ch:nozzle}). A \emph{thrust-optimized contour} (Rao's
TOC~\cite{rao}) answers a different question: for a \emph{given}
length, which shape produces the most thrust? It is shorter and
fuller, its exit flow deliberately non-uniform, and it buys more
thrust per kilogram of nozzle. Both shapes here reach the same
\emph{area ratio} $\varepsilon = A_e/A_t = 4$: exit area four times
throat area, the number saying how much the nozzle expands the gas.
Throughout, tolerances are \emph{derived bands} computed from the
discretization rather than chosen, and every test carries
\emph{rejectors}: corrupted inputs it must fail
(Chapter~\ref{ch:method}; symbols in Table~\ref{tab:notation}).

\subsection{The question}

Everything so far checks our code against itself and against closed
forms. Where is the \emph{independent} program that can referee whole
flowfields?

\subsection{How it works, in full}

GENO~\cite{geno} is that program: an independent Fortran nozzle
designer --- separate code base, separate discretization, separate
thermodynamic backend --- and, by standing decision, never modified by
us and never differentiated. As \emph{generator} it produces reference
contours and flowfields, in particular the thrust-optimized nozzle
Section~\ref{sec:o33} needs: a genuinely optimal shape our optimizer
had no hand in. As \emph{referee} its fields are put on trial against
\emph{our} equations. Our solver builds a flow on a mesh of
intersecting wave lines, enforcing a fixed set of algebraic relations
at each mesh point --- its \emph{unit process} (Chapter~\ref{ch:moc});
those relations are now evaluated on GENO's own node values, where
they must hold within the derived band. The control is a set of
deliberately wrong \emph{feet} --- the upstream points on the two wave
lines from which a mesh point is built --- which must \emph{not} hold.
Agreement then means the two codes solve the same equations cell by
cell, not just that their outputs look alike.

GENO's old binary predated this host's toolchain, so it was rebuilt
from source; the regenerated reference case is the type-2 TOC at
$\varepsilon = 4$ ($874\times2885$ nodes).

\subsection{Results of record}

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Cross-code oracle: PASS.} 218/218 sampled
  cells of the regenerated field satisfy our unit-process relations
  inside the derived band, with $84\times$ discrimination between
  correct and deliberately wrong characteristic feet.
\item \textbf{Brick 1's last SKIP closed.} A Brick-1 test was
  unrunnable while the referee would not build here. It now runs
  live, comparing our ideal contour with GENO's station by station:
  62/62 samples in band, maximum error $7.6\times10^{-9}$~m.
\item \textbf{The $f_2$ positive control landed} (the one
  declared-pending test of Section~\ref{sec:corner}). $f_2$ combines
  local flow speed and flow direction, and the theory forces it to be
  constant along one line of an optimal nozzle --- the \emph{terminal
  characteristic}, defined below. On the genuine Rao-optimal contour
  it is constant there to a relative spread of $4.0\times10^{-3}$
  while along the wall it varies by 0.60 --- a $149\times$ separation.
  The \emph{truncated} reference case correctly showed no constancy:
  cut short, an optimal contour no longer contains its own control
  surface.
\end{itemize}

\section{Rao's multipliers vs our adjoint, on a real optimal contour}
\label{sec:o33}

\subsection{The question}

Rao solved the optimal-nozzle problem in 1958 with paper and the
calculus of variations. This program solves it by \emph{automatic
differentiation}: the machine propagates derivatives through every
operation the solver performs, giving the exact sensitivity of thrust
to every shape parameter --- and run backwards (an \emph{adjoint}) one
pass returns \emph{all} of them at once, for about the cost of one
extra flow solve. The registry's oldest open item (its C-O33
in~\cite{theory}) asks whether the two answers agree, term by term, on
a real thrust-optimized contour.

\subsection{How it works, in full}

The meeting ground is GENO's optimal contour, marched by \emph{our}
prescribed-wall engine (Chapter~\ref{ch:wallinput}). When something is
maximized subject to constraints, each constraint enters the solution
with a constant that prices it --- a \emph{Lagrange multiplier}. Rao's
solution~\cite{rao} carries two: $\lambda_2$, the price of holding the
mass flow fixed, and $\lambda_3$, the price of holding the length
fixed. Each is falsifiable, because the theory names a combination of
local flow quantities that must equal it at every point of one curve
--- a \emph{first integral}, a quantity the equations keep constant
there. That curve is the \emph{terminal characteristic}: the last
$C^{-}$ wave line of the nozzle, from the axis to the lip, the control
surface the calculus of variations selects because thrust and mass
flow are evaluated across it. There:
\begin{equation}
f_2 = \frac{W\cos(\theta-\alpha)}{\cos\alpha} = -\lambda_2 ,
\qquad
2\pi y\,\rho W^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\,\tan\alpha = -\lambda_3 ,
\label{eq:firstintegrals}
\end{equation}
with $W$ the flow speed [m/s] (the $q$ of Table~\ref{tab:notation}),
$\theta$ the flow angle, $\alpha$ the Mach angle, $\rho$ the density,
$y$ the radius --- both \emph{constant along that curve} if and only
if the contour is optimal. The test samples both quantities on GENO's
own terminal-characteristic nodes and demands constancy within derived
bands in our field \emph{and} in GENO's, plus agreement between the
codes on the same nodes.

The adjoint side tests \emph{stationarity}, the calculus statement of
sitting on a peak: at a maximum the derivative in every \emph{allowed}
direction vanishes, so small allowed changes cost nothing to first
order. The allowed directions are wall deformations keeping the length
and both endpoints fixed --- six endpoint-pinned Gaussian bumps
(centers at $0.20L\ldots0.85L$). A bump moving the lip probes a
direction the theory does not protect (a bigger nozzle always pays a
little more) and must give a large gradient; the same six bumps on a
deliberately perturbed wall are the positive control. Every AD
projection is cross-checked against finite differences, i.e.\ against
recomputing the thrust with the shape nudged by hand.

\subsection{Results of record}

\textbf{9/9 PASS across two stages}
(\carrier{validation/a1\_o33\_toc.py}). \emph{Integrals}: our field
on GENO's terminal-characteristic nodes shows $f_2$ constant with
relative spread $4.045\times10^{-3}$ (GENO's own:
$4.04\times10^{-3}$), and the level matches across codes to 0.235~m/s
in 2634.7~m/s --- $9\times10^{-5}$ relative (Figure~\ref{fig:f2});
$\lambda_3$ matches pointwise (its constancy is honestly declared
unresolvable at this surface precision: $\lambda_3 \sim
\sin^{2}\theta$ is small and steep near the axis).
\emph{Stationarity}: the six interior gradients sit $1000\times$ below
the lip direction and $40\times$ below the perturbed-wall gradients,
AD${}={}$FD to ${\sim}1\%$ throughout, and the optimal contour
outscores the perturbed one, $J({\rm TOC}) > J({\rm perturbed})$
(Figure~\ref{fig:stationarity}). One rejector was itself corrected
by physics along the way: Appendix~\ref{app:f2rejector}. The formal
registry discharge (YAML and suite wiring) is a commit-time
act on the WSL repository; the numerical content exists.
