\chapter{The problems met, and how they were fixed}
\label{ch:appendix}

A document of record that showed only the working version would be
hiding its best evidence. Every test in this program is built to be
able to say \emph{no}: its pass band is derived from the scheme's
own measured error rather than chosen by hand, and each suite plants
deliberate corruptions --- \emph{rejectors} --- that must make it
fail. A bug the suite caught is therefore not an embarrassment but a
measurement: proof that the instrument has teeth, and a record of
what the physics does when it is got wrong. The main chapters show
each step as it now works; this appendix collects, step by step,
what went wrong on the way, how it was diagnosed, and what was
changed. Nothing here is anecdote --- every item was caught by a
test and closed by a measured fix.

\section{Step 1: the unclosed control surface}
\label{app:sliver}

Thrust is computed as a balance over a \emph{control surface}: an
imaginary closed surface drawn around the flow, over which the
momentum carried through and the pressure pushing on it are added
up. One test of that bookkeeping is the \emph{gauge test} --- add
the same constant to the pressure everywhere and nothing may
change, because a constant integrates to zero over a surface that
is genuinely closed. It failed on its very first run
(Chapter~\ref{ch:score}).

Cause: the wall integral of route 2 started at the first wall
\emph{point} of the mesh, leaving a microscopic sliver of wall
between the throat lip and that point uncovered. The surface was
not closed, so a constant no longer integrated to zero (the red
circle in Figure~\ref{fig:tworoutes}). Fix: close the surface at
the throat lip. The momentum residual $R_{\rm mom}$ --- the
disagreement between the two independent thrust routes --- got
\emph{tighter} once the sliver stopped hiding inside it: the bug
had been absorbing part of the mechanical balance.

\section{Step 6: the rejector corrected by physics}
\label{app:f2rejector}

A rejector is only useful if the corrupted case it poses is one the
test could plausibly fail. The first negative control for the
first-integral test of Section~\ref{sec:o33} --- the check that a
particular combination $f_2$ of speed and angle is constant along
the last characteristic of an optimal nozzle --- used an arbitrary
vertical cut through the flowfield, expecting $f_2$ to vary along
it. It barely did: much of the smooth tail of a nozzle is nearly
$f_2$-flat, so a vertical cut is a \emph{weak} negative control.
The sharp one is the \emph{wall}, a streamline along which $f_2$
climbs by $64\%$; the rejector was rebuilt on it. Lesson: even
rejectors must be chosen by physics, not by convenience.

\section{Step 4: the table-kink ripple}
\label{app:ripple}

The gradient stage of Chapter~\ref{ch:wallinput} first posed the
obvious test: compare the derivative produced by automatic
differentiation (AD) with a finite difference --- the objective
recomputed after nudging one input --- at shrinking nudge sizes,
expecting agreement to improve. It failed in a way no step size
could fix. The finite difference diverged as the step fell below
the width of one cell of the gas-property tables, while the AD
value oscillated.

Diagnosis: the tables are interpolated piecewise-linearly, so the
function the code actually implements has small kinks at every
table cell boundary, and the \emph{exact} derivative of that
function carries a genuine ripple at the table-cell scale ---
measured at a standard deviation of $44$, i.e.\ $0.010\%$ of the
gradient and $1.2\times10^{-8}$ of the thrust $J$
(Figure~\ref{fig:ripple}). Neither number was wrong; the
\emph{test} was. A pointwise AD-versus-finite-difference test on
tabulated thermodynamics is ill-posed.

The honest certificate separates the smooth curvature of $J$
(physics) from the ripple (interpolation) by fitting a straight
line over a sweep of nudge amplitudes and testing the resulting
envelope identity instead --- the certificate the suite now
carries. A first analysis also mislabeled a smooth $2.25\%$
curvature as ``ripple''; the linear-fit separation corrected that
to the true $0.010\%$. Cubic table interpolation would remove the
ripple at the source. The same trap awaits any differentiable
solver built on tabulated properties, the CFD-adjoint track
included.

\section{Step 7: three cycle-layer traps}
\label{app:cycletraps}

The cycle layer (Chapter~\ref{ch:cycle}) --- which represents an
RDE by a family of steady chamber states, one per phase $\xi$ of
the wave's passage --- met three traps, each now guarded by a
rejector.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{The enthalpy zero-point.} Enthalpy has no absolute
  zero: every table fixes its own reference state, and only
  differences are physical. Building each phase's chamber state
  \emph{absolutely}, as $h_0 = h(T_0)$ read straight off the raw
  table, mis-stated the state by $19\%$. Fix: build phase states
  \emph{relative} to the tables' own reference
  ($h_0 = h_0^{\rm tab} + h(T_0) - h(T_s^{\rm tab})$, and likewise
  for entropy with its $R\ln(P_0/p_s)$ term). Guard: doubling the
  chamber pressure must exactly double every pressure in the field,
  $p(2P_0)/p(P_0) = 2$, now verified to $1.6\times10^{-15}$.
\item \textbf{The pressure-blind peak design.} The first attempt to
  ``design at the peak phase'' held the exit Mach number fixed ---
  which is not designing at the peak at all, because it ignores
  where that phase's own exit pressure would match the ambient.
  Fix: the peak design solves $p_e(\varepsilon) = p_a$ for the peak
  phase's own state (\code{eps\_star\_at\_pa}).
\item \textbf{The silent table clamp.} A first mock-RDE run posed a
  peak chamber temperature $T_0 = 4162$~K --- past the 3900~K edge
  of the tables --- and the interpolation routine
  \code{np.interp} \emph{clamps silently} at the edge instead of
  complaining. The run completed, with quietly flattened
  thermodynamics. Fix: the mock cycle was re-posed inside table
  validity (peak 3850~K), and a table-range rejector now fails any
  phase whose $T_0$ leaves the tables --- the first thing to check
  when a measured cycle is fed in.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Step 7 (assembly): the free-jet sign trap}
\label{app:signtrap}

On the free jet edge --- the boundary where the exhaust meets the
atmosphere, on which the pressure is prescribed and the position is
solved --- the first version of the cell wrote the speed condition
by substituting moduli,
$v = +\sqrt{q_{p_a}^2 - u^2}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:freejet}b). That
form silently allows only outward turns, because the square root is
never negative; a \emph{compressing} jet must turn inward. The
unit-test rejector U-3 --- a compressing edge posed on exact data
--- caught the clamped spurious root. The cell was reformulated
with the turning \emph{angle} $\theta_4$ as the unknown, so the
sign of the turn lives inside the unknown itself and both
directions are reachable --- the formulation certified 6/6 in
Chapter~\ref{ch:plug}.

\section{The plug assembly: four structural lessons}
\label{app:pluglessons}

The plug march of Chapter~\ref{ch:plug} reached its certified form
through four measured structural failures. Each was caught by the
closed-form oracle --- an exactly known answer the march must
reproduce --- diagnosed to a mechanism, and closed by a design
change. The design presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:plug} \emph{is} the
sum of these fixes.

\subsection{Lesson 1: the degenerate edge root}
\label{app:edgeroot}

In the first assembly the free-edge cell was fed from the
\emph{previous} column, and on straight stretches of the edge the
Newton solve returned the new point exactly on top of the old one
(pt4 = pt3) --- a zero step, repeated at every column: the edge
piled up and never advanced.

The mechanism (Figure~\ref{fig:edgeroot}) is worth stating because
it is generic. Newton does not march, it \emph{solves}: anything
that zeroes the residuals is a valid root, including a useless one.
At pt4 = pt3 the streamline condition is satisfied identically
($0=0$), the pressure condition already holds (pt3 is on the free
boundary by construction), and the third equation turns on what pt3
\emph{is} in the mesh. It was created, one step earlier, as the
exit of a characteristic segment launched from the previous
column's top data --- the mesh literally records a characteristic
terminating at pt3. A sender taken from that same data therefore
lies on pt3's own characteristic (exactly so where flow and edge
are straight, since there the discrete segments lie exactly on the
straight characteristics; to second order where they curve --- which
is why the bug hid until the edge ran straight), and re-drawing
that line can only re-find its recorded exit.

The fix: sweep each column from the bottom up and feed the edge
from its \emph{own} column's top interior point --- a point on the
\emph{next} characteristic of the family, whose line is not asked
to hit pt3 at all but to cross the \emph{streamline} through pt3.
That is a shallower line, which a steeper line from below catches
strictly downstream.

\subsection{Lesson 2: rows must grow with the jet}

The first mesh carried a fixed number of rows per column. But the
jet widens downstream: the nearly horizontal characteristics of one
family dragged the fixed rows down while the free edge climbed away
from them, until the top of the mesh fell out of the jet altogether
and the two families tangled (Figure~\ref{fig:pluglessons},
panel~2). The bell march survives the same geometry by adding one
row per column; the plug march now does the mirrored same --- each
column's free-edge point becomes a new top row, so every column
brings one new characteristic and the mesh keeps filling the jet.

\subsection{Lesson 3: foot adjacency vs step accuracy}
\label{app:footbias}

With the edge and the rows fixed, the wall cell still failed its
pressure check downstream, and a probe \emph{named} the defect:
wall angles exact to four decimals everywhere, but wall speeds
biased and drifting once the segments grew long.

The first scheme confined the wall cell's \emph{foot} --- the point
on the previous column from which the arriving characteristic
departed --- to the first segment of the adjacent column. To keep
the foot there the columns had to be paced, with the axial spacing
allowed to grow up to a clamp of $0.30$. A single midpoint closure
over a chord that long, crossing the expansion fan, is
systematically biased: the chord itself lies along a straight ray
from the lip, along which the state is constant, but its end states
are interpolated \emph{across} rays on the column, and the midpoint
rule compounds that transverse error over the whole long segment.
Intermediate rounds (aligned columns, row growth, pacing) raised
the wall-pressure agreement from $22\%$ to $55\%$ of stations and
made the failure reproducible at the same station at both
resolutions --- the signature of a systematic closure defect rather
than noise.

The fix is the multi-column foot search now in the march
(Chapter~\ref{ch:plug}): columns are placed at the fine prescribed
stations, and the wall cell searches the whole previous column,
stepping further back if needed, for the segment that brackets its
arriving characteristic. Chords shrink from the $0.30$ clamp to the
station spacing, and a midpoint closure's error falls with the
square of the chord. That single change took the wall-pressure
agreement from $55\%$ to $98\%$ of stations and brought the edge
angle into band.

\subsection{Lesson 4: the ghost rows, and why one oracle is not
enough}
\label{app:ghostrows}

With the foot search in place the suite still read 3/5: every cell
Newton-certified, edge and wall pressure in band --- and mass
conservation off by $+9\%$. The probe found a whole limb of mesh
\emph{below the spike}, carrying the upstream uniform state
(Figure~\ref{fig:plugbefore}).

Mechanism: with fine stations, a near-wall row's characteristic
terminates on the wall \emph{between} two stations. The march kept
prolonging that row anyway, and for such a row the only crossing
Newton can still find is the \emph{backward} one --- a perfectly
valid root of the cell's equations, converged and certified, on the
wrong branch. (The foot search was already detecting the
terminations --- its foot row climbed one row per column --- but
nothing acted on them.) The fix is the row \emph{consumption} of
Chapter~\ref{ch:plug}: at a solid wall the incoming family
terminates, so rows of the previous column below the new foot are
retired and the column re-indexed --- the exact dual of the edge
adding one row per column. Mass then closes to $0.026\%$.

The methodological lesson is the reason this appendix exists. The
defect was invisible to the pointwise oracles (P-1, P-2) and to
Newton certification, because every individual cell really had been
solved correctly; only the \emph{global} mass balance saw it.
Suites need conservation checks precisely because wrong-branch
roots certify locally.

\label{app:plugcycleposing}

certified form through three measured corrections --- two about how
the problem must be \emph{posed}, and one where the theory itself

\subsection{The valley spike}

The first world posed the ambient pressure too low (the mean-phase
pressure at Mach $2.35$). The fan at the cowl lip then turned the
flow by $+40^{\circ}$ --- past axial --- and the streamline meant
to be the spike dived, reversed and climbed \emph{above the lip}: a
valley, not a spike, with the wall approaching the free edge and
every downstream check breaking on the geometry. The fix is the
classic plug design condition: pose the ambient so that the fan
turn roughly cancels the incoming flow angle, i.e.\
$\theta_E \approx 0$ (the flow leaves axially, adapted to the
ambient), which makes the streamline genuinely descending.

\subsection{The plug feels the ambient: the shock-free cycle bound}

A bell nozzle never feels the ambient: its flow is computed to a
fixed exit whatever the outside pressure, and $p_a$ enters only the
score. A plug feels it directly, through the free edge where
$p = p_a$ is imposed on the flow itself.

Running the certified mock cycle, whose chamber pressure ratio
between the strongest and weakest phase is $PR = 10$, against one
shared ambient put the low phases $6\times$ overexpanded already
\emph{at the start line} --- a regime with shocks, outside what an
isentropic march can represent. The failure was measured, not
hypothetical: the free-edge cells blew up and the garbage descended
row by row to the wall. Newton certification caught it, and the
scaling-law check read a spread of $14.6$ where it should have read
machine zero.

A cycle for a plug study must therefore be posed \emph{shock-free}:
every phase underexpanded at the start and adapting within the
domain. With one shared ambient that bounds the usable pressure
ratio --- here $PR = 2.5$. The theory's statements do not depend on
$PR$, and the corpus already conditions on shock-free flow; but the
bound is a real modeling limit worth stating, because cycles as
violent as $PR = 10$ put their low phases into shocked plug
operation that this engine cannot yet represent.

\subsection{The theory rejecting the wrong test, twice}

Two of the first draft's checks failed --- and both failures were
the theory working, not the code.

First, the draft transplanted the bell's collapse result (``the
cycle optimum is the design for the mean-pressure phase'') to the
plug. It failed: the measured optimum sits at $l^{*} = 2.10$
against a mean-phase design of $1.28$. That is the
\emph{anti-collapse} --- the opposite side of the theory's
dichotomy --- because the collapse result is proved only for a
fixed, fully flowing wall, which a truncated plug with a free edge
is not. The check was inverted into the anti-collapse oracle now in
Chapter~\ref{ch:plugcycle}.

Second, the draft required the per-phase votes at $l^{*}$ --- each
phase's wall pressure minus the ambient at the truncation plane ---
to vary monotonically with phase and change sign once, as certified
for the bell in Chapter~\ref{ch:cycle}. They do not: on a plug the
adaptation waves make the wall pressure oscillate about $p_a$, so
pointwise votes sample an oscillation and the wave-passage phase
can vote negative at a length below its own optimum. The theory
claims only the averaged balance and the compromise structure, and
exactly those pass; the over-specified checks were replaced by
theorem-faithful ones (a genuine spread of the per-phase optima;
$l^{*}$ a strict interior compromise).

The lesson generalizes: an oracle may be transplanted from one
problem class to another only together with the hypotheses that
prove it.
\section{Step 15: two instruments, one constraint}
\label{app:twoinstruments}

The inlet-angle sweep held its mass constraint by a root search: move
the spike's start radius until the flow crossing the start line
carries the reference mass flow. It then graded itself by measuring
that same mass flow. For several sessions the grade came back at
$7\times10^{-5}$ relative --- small, but an order above the band the
refinement supported, so the check failed and the failure resisted
the obvious fixes. Widening the band would have buried it; refining
the quadrature did nothing.

The residue was not an error at all. The constraint was being
\emph{set} with one rule and \emph{graded} with another. Sizing used
the trapezoid of the product $\rho\, u\, 2\pi y$; the verdict used
the march's own column functional, which multiplies midpoint means,
$\rho_m u_m\, 2\pi y_m$. Both are second-order, both are correct, and
they differ on every interval by a quarter of the product of the
increments --- which on $61$ rows is exactly the $7\times10^{-5}$
that would not go away.

Sizing with the functional that grades removes it at the source: the
mass residue falls to between $10^{-15}$ and $3\times10^{-10}$, i.e.
the constraint is satisfied to machine precision, and the derived
band ($2.9\times10^{-4}$) is no longer being asked to cover a
disagreement between two instruments.

A second failure dissolved with it. One outward-turning candidate had
been failing Newton certification at a worst residual of $6.5$, and
had been read as a marching limit of that configuration. It was not:
with the annulus correctly sized, the same candidate certifies at
$0.05$. A mis-set constraint had been quietly deforming the geometry
that the march was then blamed for.

The lesson is the sharper form of the one in
Chapter~\ref{ch:compare}: it is not enough for a constraint and its
verdict to measure the same \emph{quantity}. They must be the same
\emph{functional}. Two correct rules for one integral will disagree
at their common order of accuracy, and that disagreement will present
itself as a physical defect.
\section{Step 16: three ways to mismeasure with two points}
\label{app:twopoints}

Building the free-form spike produced the same error three times in
one session, in three disguises. It is worth stating once.

\emph{First}, the check that the spline basis can represent the
contour it starts from compared two knot counts and read their ratio
as a convergence rate. The ratio was $1.5\times$, far too slow for
cubic interpolation, and the check failed. But two competing errors
are present --- one at the spline's free right end, one at the
station where the contour actually bends --- and which of them
dominates alternates as knots are added. Two adjacent counts sample
whichever happens to be winning. Across a ladder of four counts the
basis reduces its error by $28\times$ and is perfectly adequate.

\emph{Second}, the mass constraint was set with one quadrature rule
and graded with another (Appendix~\ref{app:twoinstruments}). Two
correct second-order rules for the same integral disagree at their
common order, and that disagreement presented itself as a physical
defect that no amount of refinement removed.

\emph{Third}, the adjoint was graded against a central difference
whose error band was estimated by halving the step once. That
estimate assumes the error is truncation. Here it is not: the
replayed march is only piecewise smooth in the design vector, so the
difference carries noise that does not shrink with the step. Measured
across five steps the discrepancy wanders instead of falling as
$h^2$. The two-point band therefore described nothing, and a correct
adjoint was reported as a failure.

The common structure is this. \textbf{Two points can measure a
difference, but never a rate, and never the reliability of the
instrument that produced them.} Wherever this program grades something
against a derived band, the band must come from a ladder --- three
points at least --- and the ladder must be inspected rather than
reduced to a single ratio. Where the underlying quantity is not
smooth, the ladder measures scatter rather than truncation, and that
scatter \emph{is} the honest band.

The corollary is worth keeping too: all three failures pointed at the
wrong culprit. In the first the basis was blamed, in the second the
flow, in the third the adjoint. In every case the instrument was at
fault, and in every case the instrument was the thing not being
measured.
\section{The referee's own defects, and a binary that could not be rebuilt}
\label{app:geno}

Posing the classical construction in this document's world
(Section~\ref{sec:vsrao}) required running the independent Fortran
code at a mass and a length it had never been asked for, and that
exposed three defects and one environment failure. They are recorded
because two of the three were ours, and because the environment
failure is the kind that invalidates a referee silently.

\emph{A NaN where a refusal belonged.} The code's design-curve
integrator can exhaust its step budget without reaching a corner, an
axis or its mass target; that is base behaviour and it used to degrade
quietly. The mass accumulator this program added propagated straight
through it and returned a mass of NaN. A caller then compared that NaN
against a target and got a residual that is neither positive nor
negative, which silently poisoned a bisection and finally surfaced as
a NaN entropy in the thermodynamics. The fix tests the increments
\emph{before} accumulating --- adding and then subtracting a NaN does
not undo it --- and stops with a distinct reason the caller can
reject.

\emph{A solution to the wrong problem.} With the guard in place the
length bisection ran, and returned a design that met the length exactly
while missing the mass by $63.6\%$: it had found the root on a branch
where the curve stops at its corner instead of at its mass target.
Both roots exist; only one solves the two-constraint problem that was
posed. The validity test now requires termination on mass whenever
mass matching is on.

\emph{A budget mistaken for a limit.} The step cap of $500$ was
binding on long curves. The design that expands to this document's
ambient runs to $5.8$ m and needs several thousand steps; at $500$ it
stopped mid-curve and reported whatever length it had reached, which
is why the classical spike for our conditions appeared not to exist.
Raising the cap cannot affect a curve that already terminates, and the
reference case reproducing exactly is what proves it.

\emph{The binary could not be rebuilt.} The working executable had
been compiled with a compiler and a linear-algebra library that are no
longer installed. Rebuilding from the same source with the current
toolchain produces a binary that \textbf{fails the code's own
reference case} --- the exit-angle scan collapses and the prescribed
exit Mach becomes unreachable --- while the same source at $-O0$
reproduces the reference exactly. The code is therefore
optimization-fragile under the current compiler, in a way its previous
one tolerated.

The attribution matters more than the fix. The failure appeared
immediately after our edits, and the obvious inference was that we had
broken it. What settled the question was removing our edits entirely
and rebuilding: the failure reproduced identically. Only then was it
legitimate to say the compiler was at fault --- and only then was it
safe to keep the fix.

\section{An instrument that guessed instead of refusing}
\label{app:silentclamp}

The field meter of Chapter~\ref{ch:status} measures mass and momentum
through a vertical cut, and its first version reported a mass excess
of $6$--$7\%$ that decayed downstream. The shape of the error was
persuasive: it looked like a near-field resolution defect, and the
obvious suspect --- the first marched column's known coverage gap ---
was tested first. Filling that gap changed nothing at all, at any
setting.

The cause was that the meter did not know where the jet was. A
characteristics march emits free-boundary points only once the
marching front reaches the boundary, so its boundary polyline begins
at $x = 2.69$ and does not exist upstream of that. The routine that
looks up the jet radius used a standard interpolation, which
\emph{clamps} silently outside its data range and returned the
polyline's first value as though it were the local radius. Every cut
upstream then spanned a phantom outer band, and because area goes as
$y^{2}$, an extra $0.07$ m of radius at $y \approx 2.3$ is several
percent of the mass.

The diagnosis was a single number: at a station essentially on the
start line, where the answer is known by construction to twelve
digits, the meter read $+6.35\%$. An instrument that is wrong where
the answer is known is wrong everywhere.

The repair is not a better guess but a refusal: the lookup now raises
outside its span. Restricted to the window where the boundary actually
exists, the mass residual is $0.6$--$1.0\%$ rather than $7\%$. An
