\chapter{The score and the compass}
\label{ch:score}

The solver of Brick~1 answers ``what does the gas do in this nozzle?''.
An optimizer needs two further things: a single number that says how
good a nozzle is, and the derivative of that number with respect to
every shape knob, which tells it which way to move. This chapter builds
both and, more importantly, makes both checkable --- the number by
computing it twice along two completely different paths, the derivative
by comparing it with brute-force re-runs of the solver. It then adds a
second instrument that does not score a design but \emph{certifies} it:
a dial that reads zero exactly when the nozzle wall ends where an
optimal wall must end.

\section{The thrust functional, by two routes}

\subsection{The question}

An optimizer needs exactly two things the flow solver did not have.
First a \emph{number} that says how good one design is --- the
\emph{objective} $J$, here the thrust in newtons, the axial force the
nozzle produces. Second its \emph{gradient}: the list of partial
derivatives of that number with respect to each design knob, i.e.\ how
much $J$ changes per metre of change in each knob. The gradient is the
compass; it points uphill.

\subsection{How it works, in full}

Thrust is a \emph{momentum balance}: Newton's second law applied not to
a body but to a region of gas. Draw a \emph{control surface} --- an
imaginary closed surface enclosing the nozzle flow --- and add up two
things on it: the momentum the gas carries through the surface, and the
pressure force pushing on it. The sum is the force the machine feels.
Nothing physical depends on where that surface is drawn, and that
freedom is exploited twice, by computing $J$ on two surfaces that share
no data:
\begin{align}
J &= \dot m\, u_e + (p_e - p_a)\, A_e
  &&\text{(route 1: read the exit),} \label{eq:route1}\\
J &= F_{\rm throat} + \int_{\rm wall} (p - p_a)\, 2\pi y \,\mathrm{d}y
  &&\text{(route 2: read the wall).} \label{eq:route2}
\end{align}
Route 1 closes the surface at the exit plane, where an ideal nozzle
delivers flow that is uniform and parallel to the axis. There the
balance is elementary: the mass flow $\dot m$ [kg/s] times the exit
speed $u_e$ [m/s], plus the mismatch between the exit static pressure
$p_e$ and the ambient (surrounding) pressure $p_a$ acting on the exit
area $A_e$.

Route 2 closes the same surface along the machine itself. Its first
term is the momentum flux entering through the \emph{throat} --- the
narrowest cross-section, where the gas reaches exactly the local speed
of sound. A throat in that state is called \emph{choked}: the mass flow
through it is then set by the throat area and the chamber conditions
alone, and nothing downstream can increase it. The carrier evaluates
this flux on the march's initial-value line, the short line across the
throat where the solver starts. The second term is the axial push of
the wall: every ring of wall of radius $y$ and radial width
$\mathrm{d}y$ presents an axial projected area $2\pi y\,\mathrm{d}y$ on
which the pressure excess $(p - p_a)$ pushes forward. The two routes
share no intermediate quantity: route~1 never reads a wall pressure,
route~2 never reads the exit state.

Newton's law forces the two answers to agree, so their difference
$R_{\rm mom}$ [N] is a mechanical error meter --- if the discretization
is wrong anywhere, the two routes stop matching. How large is it
allowed to be? Not a number chosen by taste. The march is run twice, at
one resolution and at double resolution; the \emph{change} in
$R_{\rm mom}$ between the two runs is the scheme's own estimate of its
discretization error. That change, multiplied by the safety factor
$K_{\rm RICH} = 4$ and added to a floor for floating-point roundoff, is
the allowed band --- a \emph{Richardson band}, named after the
two-resolution extrapolation idea it comes from.

Two further instruments complete the step. The \emph{gauge test}: add
the same constant to the pressure everywhere and the thrust must not
move, because a constant pressure integrates to zero over any closed
surface. That is an identity of the \emph{formulas}, not of the flow,
so it isolates errors in the surface bookkeeping from errors in the
flow --- and it caught one (Appendix~\ref{app:sliver}). And the
\emph{gradient}: because the march is written in a differentiable
language, \emph{reverse-mode automatic differentiation} (AD) --- the
technique that differentiates the program itself, propagating
derivatives backwards through every operation it performed, which is
mathematically the same as solving the discrete adjoint equations ---
returns $\mathrm{d}J/\mathrm{d}P$ for all design parameters
$P = (y_t, r_{tu}, r_{td}, \varepsilon)$ in a single backward pass, at
about the cost of one extra march. Each of the four components is then
checked against a \emph{central finite difference}: re-run the march
with that knob nudged up by $h$ and down by $h$, and divide the
difference of the two thrusts by $2h$. Halving $h$ gives the second
estimate the Richardson band is built from.

\subsection{What was built}

\carrier{validation/a1\_thrust\_functional.py}: both routes, the
momentum residual $R_{\rm mom}$ with its Richardson band, the gauge
test, reverse-mode $\mathrm{d}J/\mathrm{d}P$ checked against central
finite differences knob by knob, and three rejectors --- deliberately
corrupted versions of the computation (dropped pressure--area term,
un-closed surface, corrupted flux) that the suite must catch.

\subsection{Results of record}

\textbf{8/8 PASS.} $J = 1.2552079\times10^{8}$~N; $R_{\rm mom} =
+2.85\times10^{3}$ against a derived band of $4.01\times10^{4}$
($3.2\times10^{-4}$ of $J$), so the two routes agree to about three
parts in ten thousand of the thrust; the coarse/fine residual ratio of
4.52 is the $\approx 4$ expected of a second-order scheme when the
resolution is doubled, confirming that what is left is discretization
error and not a mistake. Gauge delta $3.35\times10^{-8}$ vs.\ allowed
$1.78\times10^{-6}$: adding a constant pressure really does change
nothing. All four gradient components fall inside their
finite-difference bands ($\mathrm{d}J/\mathrm{d}y_t$ agreeing to 9
significant figures), and all rejectors fire.

The gradient signs also behave as theory predicts:
$\mathrm{d}J/\mathrm{d}\varepsilon > 0$ against a vacuum, i.e.\ with
nothing pushing back a longer nozzle is always worth building; and
$\mathrm{d}J/\mathrm{d}r_{td}$ is tiny, i.e.\ the curvature of the
short arc just downstream of the throat barely matters. One real bug
was caught and fixed on the way: Appendix~\ref{app:sliver}.

\section{The optimality meter}
\label{sec:corner}

\subsection{The question}

A score and a compass find better designs. What \emph{certifies} that a
given design already satisfies the condition an optimum must satisfy?

\subsection{How it works, in full}

Choosing the best nozzle wall is not a problem of finding the best
value of a few numbers but of finding the best \emph{curve}; the branch
of mathematics for that is the \emph{calculus of variations}, which
asks what must be true of a curve if no small deformation of it can
improve the objective. Rao~\cite{rao} solved that problem for a nozzle
and found, among the conditions, one exact scalar statement that must
hold at the lip --- the point where the wall stops. It is an
\emph{endpoint} (or transversality) condition:
\begin{equation}
R_{\rm corner}(p_a) \;=\; (p - p_a)\;-\;\tfrac{1}{2}\rho W^{2}
\sin(2\theta)\tan\alpha .
\label{eq:corner}
\end{equation}
Read it as the balance of the two effects of moving the lip one step
further downstream. The first term is the direct gain: one more ring of
wall, worth building as long as the pressure on it exceeds ambient. The
second is the price: the extra wall forces the flow to keep turning,
and the cost of that turning is written through the local flow angle
$\theta$ (the direction of the velocity relative to the axis) and the
Mach angle $\alpha$ (the tilt of the lines along which supersonic
disturbances travel, Chapter~\ref{ch:moc}). $R_{\rm corner} = 0$ means
``the wall stops in exactly the right place'': one more ring would gain
as much as it costs. The meter evaluates this from the march's own lip
data, so any design the engine produces can be certified and not merely
scored. Averaged over the engine cycle it becomes the lip condition
that a rotating detonation engine's nozzle must satisfy (statement T7
of~\cite{theory}), which Chapter~\ref{ch:cycle} puts on a meter of its
own.

The companion instrument is the \emph{first integral}
\begin{equation}
f_2 \;=\; \frac{W\cos(\theta-\alpha)}{\cos\alpha} \;=\; -\lambda_2 ,
\label{eq:f2}
\end{equation}
where a first integral means a combination of flow quantities that the
governing equations force to stay constant along some particular curve,
even though each ingredient varies. Rao's theory makes $f_2$ constant
along the \emph{terminal characteristic} of an optimal nozzle --- the
last of the two families of lines along which supersonic information
travels, the one that closes the flowfield at the lip. Its constant
value is $-\lambda_2$, a \emph{Lagrange multiplier}: the number that
prices a constraint in a constrained optimization, here telling how
much thrust would be gained per extra unit of mass flow allowed. Along
any other curve --- in particular along the wall, which is a
\emph{streamline}, a line everywhere tangent to the velocity and hence
the path a gas particle follows --- $f_2$ is \emph{not} constant. That
supplies the natural negative control: an implementation that made
$f_2$ look constant everywhere would be broken, and must be caught.

\subsection{What was built}

\carrier{validation/a1\_corner\_instrument.py}: the corner meter and
the $f_2$ instrument, tested against three known answers on the
ideal-nozzle case (axial exit, matched ambient, vacuum), with the
non-constancy rejector on the wall.

\subsection{Results of record}

\textbf{5/5 PASS} (82~s). The flow angle at the lip is machine-zero
($\theta = -10^{-16}$~rad: the exit really is axial, as the ideal
nozzle assumes). With the ambient set equal to the exit pressure the
meter reads $3.7\times10^{-10}$ against an allowed 42.8~Pa --- the
nozzle is certified stationary at its design ambient. Against a vacuum
it reads exactly $+p_e$, i.e.\ ``lengthen me'', the statement that no
finite nozzle is optimal in vacuum, now on a dial; and its sign agrees
with an independently recomputed $\mathrm{d}J/\mathrm{d}\varepsilon$,
so meter and gradient tell the same story. The first integral $f_2$ at
the lip equals the exit speed to $4.6\times10^{-13}$~m/s, while along
the wall it varies by 1173~m/s --- the non-constancy rejector, firing
as designed.
