\chapter{How the solver computes}
\label{ch:moc}

The rest of this document talks constantly about ``cells'',
``columns'', ``feet'', ``characteristics'' and ``compatibility''.
This chapter defines all of them from nothing. It describes the
\emph{method of characteristics}, the numerical method the whole
program rests on, and it is worth the four pages: almost every
technical lesson recorded later --- including every bug in the
appendix --- is a statement about the objects introduced here.

\section{The one physical fact the method exploits}

In a gas moving \emph{slower} than sound, a disturbance spreads in
every direction, upstream included: the whole flow field is coupled
to itself, and solving it means solving everywhere at once. In a
gas moving \emph{faster} than sound, it cannot. A disturbance made
at a point is swept downstream faster than it can propagate, so its
influence is confined to a wedge opening downstream of that point.

That wedge has a precise half-angle, the \emph{Mach angle}
$\alpha$, given by
\begin{equation}
\sin\alpha = \frac{1}{M}, \qquad M = \frac{q}{c},
\label{eq:machangle}
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the flow speed, $c$ the local speed of sound, and $M$
their ratio, the \emph{Mach number}. Faster flow means a narrower
wedge. Its two edges, drawn through the point, are the
\emph{characteristics}: one at angle $\theta + \alpha$ to the axis
and one at $\theta - \alpha$, where $\theta$ is the direction the
gas is actually flowing (Figure~\ref{fig:mocprimer}a). By
convention the first family is called $C^{+}$ and the second
$C^{-}$.

Characteristics are not just geometry. Along each of them the flow
must satisfy a \emph{compatibility relation}: an ordinary
differential equation tying the change in speed to the change in
flow direction, with an extra term in axisymmetric flow accounting
for the passage opening out as the radius grows. That is the
entire physical content the solver uses. Two families of lines,
one relation along each --- no volume grid, no time stepping, no
iteration to steady state.

\section{The unit process: one cell}

The method turns those relations into arithmetic with a single
repeating move, the \emph{cell} (or \emph{unit process}), shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:mocprimer}b.

Suppose two nearby points are already solved: position and velocity
known at both. From the lower one, draw its $C^{+}$ characteristic;
from the upper one, its $C^{-}$. In supersonic flow these two lines
converge and cross at exactly one point downstream. That crossing
gives two equations, one per line, for the two coordinates of the
new point. The two compatibility relations, integrated along the
two short segments just drawn, give two more equations for the two
velocity components there. Four equations, four unknowns
$(x, y, u, v)$.

The equations are nonlinear --- the characteristic directions
depend on the very state being solved for --- so each cell is
closed by a small Newton iteration. Two details of how they are
closed matter later:

\emph{Midpoint coefficients.} The coefficients of the
compatibility relations are evaluated not at the segment's start
but at its \emph{midpoint}, using the average of the two endpoint
states. This is the trapezoidal rule; it makes the error per cell
shrink with the square of the step rather than linearly, which is
why the whole march is called second order. It is also the reason
a cell must never span a region where the state changes abruptly:
averaging the two ends of such a segment is then meaningless
(Chapter~\ref{ch:plug} contains a measured example).

\emph{Newton certification.} Once a cell's Newton iteration
converges, the program checks that the residual of the four
equations really has fallen to the level of floating-point
roundoff, scaled to the size of the unknowns. A cell that stalls
is caught and reported rather than quietly polluting the march.
Every ``all cells Newton-certified'' claim in this document is that
check, run over every cell.

\section{Columns, boundaries, and feet}

New points grow out of solved points, so a march organizes itself
into \emph{columns}: from one solved column, the interior cells
produce the next column, and the sweep repeats downstream
(Figure~\ref{fig:mocprimer}c). A march therefore needs somewhere to
begin --- a \emph{start line} (or initial-value line) across the
flow, on which the complete state is supplied as data. In a rocket
nozzle that line sits just downstream of the throat.

At the edges of the flow the cell changes shape, because one of its
two parent characteristics is missing and a boundary condition
takes its place. Three boundaries appear in this document.

\emph{A symmetry axis}: the flow cannot cross it, which supplies
the missing relation.

\emph{A prescribed wall}: the new point's position is already
known, because it must lie on a given contour with a given slope.
The cell then works \emph{backwards}. It must find the
\emph{foot} --- the point on the previous column from which the
arriving characteristic departed --- which in general falls
between two stored points and must be located by searching along
the column and interpolating. The compatibility relation is then
closed along the chord from that foot to the wall point. This is
the cell that lets a designer hand in an arbitrary wall
(Chapter~\ref{ch:wallinput}), and the accuracy of the foot search
is a recurring theme.

\emph{A free jet boundary}: the case with no wall at all, where
the exhaust is bounded by the atmosphere. Here the situation is
exactly reversed: the \emph{pressure} is known (it is $p_a$, since
nothing but the atmosphere pushes on the jet) while the
\emph{position} of the boundary is unknown and must be solved.
This is the one genuinely new piece of physics the plug nozzle
needs, and Chapter~\ref{ch:plug} builds and certifies it.

\section{Sharp corners: the one thing a cell cannot do}

Where a wall turns a sharp corner, the flow expands through a
\emph{centered expansion fan}: a continuous spread of
characteristics all emanating from that single geometric point,
each carrying a different state. At the corner itself the flow is
therefore multivalued and the gradients are infinite.

No ordinary cell can step across such a fan, for both of the
reasons given above: a segment spanning the fan would average
states that differ by the entire turn, and near the corner the
characteristic crossing that fixes the new position degenerates
because all the lines meet at one point. The cure is that a
centered fan does not need to be marched --- it is
\emph{self-similar}, so the state on each ray is known in closed
form from the ray angle alone. The corner is therefore
\emph{constructed} as data and ordinary cells resume between the
rays, where the flow is smooth again. Chapter~\ref{ch:plug} shows
what happens when this rule is violated, with the measured number.

\section{Real gas, and where the derivatives come from}

Two implementation choices are worth stating once here.

The gas is not assumed to be calorically perfect. Thermodynamic
properties come from tabulated data, and the state is recovered
from the speed through the energy and entropy relations. This
matters in one non-obvious way that Chapter~\ref{ch:wallinput}
documents: tabulated properties are interpolated piecewise, so the
\emph{exact} derivative of the code carries a small ripple at the
scale of the table cells --- a real property of the discrete
objective, not a bug, but one that makes a naive derivative test
ill-posed.

And the entire march --- every cell, every Newton iteration, every
foot search --- is written so that it can be differentiated
automatically. That is what turns a solver into the engine of an
optimizer, and it is the subject of everything that follows.
