\chapter{What a nozzle does, and what ``optimal'' means}
\label{ch:nozzle}

This chapter assumes no background at all. It explains what the
machine under study is, what it is for, which numbers describe it,
why there is anything to optimize, and what makes rotating
detonation engines a harder design problem than ordinary rockets.
A reader who already designs nozzles can skip to
Chapter~\ref{ch:moc}; everyone else should read it, because every
later chapter uses this vocabulary without re-explaining it.

\section{The machine}

A rocket burns propellant in a chamber, producing gas at high
pressure and high temperature but going nowhere in particular. The
nozzle is the part that converts that stored pressure into
directed, high-speed exhaust --- and by Newton's third law, the
reaction to throwing that exhaust backwards is thrust.

The shape that does this is the \emph{converging--diverging}
nozzle of Figure~\ref{fig:nozzleprimer}a: the passage narrows to a
minimum area, the \emph{throat}, then opens out again. The
narrowing accelerates the gas to exactly the local speed of sound
at the throat; past the throat, in supersonic flow, the behaviour
inverts --- widening the passage accelerates the gas further. So
the diverging part, which is what this document designs, keeps
converting pressure into speed all the way to the exit.

Three consequences matter throughout.

\emph{The throat sets the mass flow.} When the flow reaches the
speed of sound at the throat, the passage is \emph{choked}: the
mass of gas per second, $\dot m$, is fixed by the throat area and
the chamber state, and nothing downstream can change it. Holding
the throat radius $y_t$ fixed is therefore the same thing as
holding the mass flow fixed --- a fact this document uses as a
design constraint.

\emph{The chamber state is quoted as a stagnation state.} The
chamber pressure $P_0$ and temperature $T_0$ (the \emph{stagnation}
values --- what the gas would have if brought to rest) are the
boundary data of the whole problem. Everything downstream is the
same gas, expanded.

\emph{The exit is a compromise with the outside world.} The gas
leaves at some pressure $p_e$, into an atmosphere at pressure
$p_a$. These need not be equal, and whether they are is exactly
what makes design non-trivial.

\section{The numbers that describe a design}

Only a handful of quantities are needed to state a design problem,
and this document keeps to them (the full symbol list is
Table~\ref{tab:notation}).

The \emph{area ratio} $\varepsilon = A_e/A_t$ --- exit area divided
by throat area --- is the single most important number: it says how
far the gas is expanded. A larger $\varepsilon$ means a longer,
wider nozzle, a faster and colder exhaust, and a lower exit
pressure. The \emph{wall contour} is the shape itself, the curve
$y(x)$ from throat to exit. The \emph{length} $L$ is where that
curve stops. And $p_a$, the ambient pressure, is the environment:
about 1~bar at sea level, essentially zero in vacuum.

\section{Thrust, and why there is an optimum}

Thrust is a momentum balance. Written at the exit plane it is
\begin{equation}
J \;=\; \dot m\,u_e \;+\; (p_e - p_a)\,A_e ,
\label{eq:thrustintro}
\end{equation}
the first term being the momentum carried out by the exhaust
(mass flow times exit speed) and the second the net pressure force
on the exit area. Chapter~\ref{ch:score} computes this two
independent ways as a self-check, but the physics of the optimum is
already visible in~\eqref{eq:thrustintro}.

Suppose the nozzle is made slightly longer, adding one thin ring of
wall. Gas inside pushes on that ring with pressure $p$; the
atmosphere pushes back with $p_a$. The net gain is proportional to
$p - p_a$. So while the exhaust is still at higher pressure than
its surroundings --- the flow is \emph{under-expanded} --- extra
length pays. Once the flow has expanded past ambient ---
\emph{over-expanded} --- each additional ring pushes the wrong way
and costs thrust. The best design sits exactly at the changeover:
\begin{equation}
p_e(\varepsilon^{*}) \;=\; p_a .
\label{eq:matchintro}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:nozzleprimer}c is that statement, measured. This
single idea --- extend the wall while the local pressure exceeds
ambient, stop when it does not --- reappears throughout the
document in increasingly general form: as an endpoint condition at
the nozzle lip (Chapter~\ref{ch:score}), as a condition averaged
over a cycle (Chapter~\ref{ch:cycle}), and as the rule that decides
where to cut a plug nozzle short (Chapter~\ref{ch:plugcycle}).

An important special case: in vacuum, $p_a = 0$, so $p - p_a$ is
positive everywhere and \emph{longer is always better}. There is no
interior optimum, only whatever length the vehicle can carry. Any
optimizer worth trusting must reproduce that behaviour, and several
tests in this document check precisely that.

\section{Three families of nozzle, and why the third is hard}

\emph{The ideal (minimum-length) nozzle} is the textbook shape:
its contour is drawn so that the exhaust comes out perfectly
uniform and parallel. It is easy to characterize and serves here as
a reference; Brick~1 generates it.

\emph{The thrust-optimized contour}, following Rao's 1958
solution~\cite{rao}, gives up the perfectly uniform exit and asks a
better question: of all walls of a \emph{given length}, which
produces the most thrust? The answer is a shorter, fuller shape,
and it is what real rockets use. Rao solved it with the calculus of
variations --- the mathematics of optimizing over a whole curve
rather than over a few numbers --- and Chapter~\ref{ch:referee}
confronts his hand-derived conditions with this program's automatic
machinery on exactly such a contour.

\emph{The plug (or aerospike) nozzle} turns the geometry inside
out (Figure~\ref{fig:bellvsplug}). Instead of the gas being
enclosed by a wall, the solid surface --- the \emph{spike} --- sits
underneath, and the outer boundary of the exhaust is nothing but
the surrounding atmosphere. That free boundary is what makes the
plug interesting: it is not fixed by the hardware, so the plume
re-shapes itself when the ambient pressure changes. A bell is
optimal at one altitude; a plug is roughly right over a range,
because the exhaust always ends up at exactly $p_a$ on its outer
edge, by definition. Figure~\ref{fig:bellvsplug}c shows this
computed. Real plugs are always \emph{truncated} --- cut short and
closed with a flat base --- because the long thin tail carries
almost no pressure but all of the weight, and where to cut is a
genuine design variable (Chapter~\ref{ch:plugcycle}).

\section{The rotating detonation engine, and the cycle}
\label{sec:rdeintro}

Everything above assumed one operating point: one chamber pressure,
one ambient. A \emph{rotating detonation engine} (RDE) breaks that
assumption, and it is the reason this document has a cycle layer.

In an RDE, combustion does not sit still and burn steadily. A
detonation --- a combustion wave travelling faster than sound ---
runs continuously \emph{around} an annular chamber, lapping it a
few thousand times a second (Figure~\ref{fig:rdecycle}a). Fresh
propellant is injected behind the wave and consumed on the next
lap. The attraction is thermodynamic: detonative combustion raises
the pressure as it burns, so an RDE promises more work from the
same propellant than steady burning does.

The consequence for the nozzle is severe. A fixed cross-section of
the exhaust does not see a constant chamber state; it sees the
pressure spike as the wave sweeps past and then decay until the
next lap --- over and over, thousands of times a second. What the
nozzle is handed is therefore not one stagnation state but a
\emph{periodic family} of them, $(P_0(\xi), T_0(\xi))$, labelled by
the position in the cycle, the \emph{phase} $\xi \in [0,1]$
(Figure~\ref{fig:rdecycle}b).

Because gas crosses the nozzle far faster than the chamber
conditions change, each phase can be treated as its own steady
flow, and the performance of the whole cycle is the average of the
steady performances over the phases. That assumption is called
\emph{quasi-steady}; it is what makes the problem tractable with
the machinery of this document, it is stated explicitly wherever it
is used, and its limits are discussed honestly in
Section~\ref{sec:spiral}.

The design difficulty is then immediate and is the whole subject of
Chapters~\ref{ch:cycle} and~\ref{ch:plugcycle}: each phase would
want a different nozzle (Figure~\ref{fig:rdecycle}c), and only one
piece of hardware gets built. What is the right compromise --- and
is it simply the nozzle for the average chamber condition? For one
kind of nozzle the answer turns out to be yes, exactly; for another
it is emphatically no. Measuring both is what this document does.

\section{What ``optimizing'' means here, and what an adjoint buys}

An \emph{optimizer} needs three things a flow solver does not have.
A \emph{score}: one number saying how good a design is --- here the
thrust $J$, built in Chapter~\ref{ch:score}. A \emph{compass}: the
derivative of that score with respect to every design variable, the
\emph{gradient}, which says which way to move. And a
\emph{certificate}: a condition that recognizes an optimum when it
is reached, so that ``the search stopped'' and ``the design is
optimal'' are different statements.

The gradient is where the technology of Brick~1 matters. If a
design has only one knob, the gradient can be estimated by
\emph{finite differences} --- change the knob a little, see how $J$
changes. With a whole wall shape as the unknown, that costs one
extra flow solution per degree of freedom, which is hopeless.
\emph{Automatic differentiation} (AD) avoids this. Because the
solver is written in a language that records the arithmetic it
performs, the chain rule can be applied backwards through the
entire computation, yielding the derivative of $J$ with respect to
\emph{all} inputs at once, for roughly the cost of one extra
solve, and to machine accuracy rather than to the accuracy of a
difference quotient. This backwards pass is mathematically the same
object as the \emph{discrete adjoint} of classical optimal-control
theory --- hence the name of this work package --- but it is
obtained for free rather than derived and coded by hand.

That is the engine. The rest of the document builds an optimizer
around it, one certified piece at a time.
