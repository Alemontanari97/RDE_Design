\chapter{The truncated plug under the cycle}
\label{ch:plugcycle}

Chapter~\ref{ch:plug} can march one plug at one chamber condition.
A rotating detonation engine never offers one condition: its
detonation wave sweeps the annulus and a fixed cross-section sees
the chamber pressure spike and blow down, over and over. This
chapter cuts the spike short --- real plugs are always truncated,
because the long thin tail weighs a great deal and produces almost
no thrust --- and asks where to cut when the chamber pressure
swings like that. The answer turns out not to be ``design for the
average condition'', and the difference is measurable.

\section{The question}

Two nozzle types behave in opposite ways over a cycle. The fixed
bell \emph{averages}: its cycle optimum is the design for the
single mean-pressure condition, which the engine certified in
Chapter~\ref{ch:cycle}. The full plug instead \emph{maximizes}:
every phase's optimum contains every longer plug, so the ideal plug
is sized at the strongest phase. The \emph{truncated} plug is where
both mechanisms fail at once --- ``the first genuinely averaged
shape problem''~\cite{theory}. Can the engine measure all three
claims: the ordering, its break, and the averaged optimality
condition that replaces it?

\section{How it works, in full}

\subsection{What the theory claims, and what it assumes}

Two pieces of vocabulary first. A \emph{cycle phase}
$\xi \in [0,1]$ labels where you are in the repeating chamber
history --- $\xi$ near the wave passage is the high-pressure part,
$\xi$ near the end of the blowdown is the low-pressure part
(Chapter~\ref{ch:cycle}); the cycle mean $\mu$ is the time-like
average over those phases. And a plug is \emph{truncated} at length
$l$ when the spike is cut off there and closed with a flat base:
the whole design question is what $l$ should be.

The corpus's plug result (its T-T4) is proved under an
\emph{ideal-adaptation} closure --- an assumption made to keep the
proof tractable: that downstream of each phase's full-expansion
point the wall pressure clamps exactly to the ambient $p_a$, and
upstream of it scales with the chamber pressure. Under that
assumption the thrust of a given phase, $F(l;\xi)$, never decreases
as the plug is extended and becomes exactly constant beyond a
phase-dependent length $l(\xi)$, with $l(\xi)$ larger for stronger
phases. Each phase's set of optimal lengths is then a half-line
$[l(\xi), \infty)$, and these half-lines are \emph{nested}: the
strongest phase's half-line sits inside all the others. Nesting is
what makes the problem collapse --- one length is optimal for
everybody at once, so the best average equals the average of the
bests,
$\max_\Sigma \int F\,\mathrm{d}\mu = \int \max F\,\mathrm{d}\mu$,
attained by the untruncated plug sized at the peak phase.

The sharpness caveat attached to that theorem says what destroys
the nesting: a cap on length, a base-pressure model, or adaptation
that is not ideal. Once nesting is gone the equality becomes a
strict inequality, $\max\int < \int\max$, and the optimum instead
satisfies an \emph{averaged corner condition} at the truncation
plane --- the per-phase wall-pressure votes there must balance in
the cycle mean. What the engine adds is precisely the quantity the
closure assumes away: the real marched wall pressure does not
clamp. Downstream of adaptation it undershoots and overshoots
$p_a$, and the size of that departure \emph{is} the adaptation
break, measurable phase by phase.

\subsection{Averaging the problem is not averaging the condition}
\label{sec:avgnotavg}

The cycle mean $\mu$ is a time-like average over the phases, and it
is tempting to conclude that the ``$\mu$-averaged corner
condition'' is just the mean-pressure design in disguise. It is
not, and the distinction is the whole content of the dichotomy.
\emph{Mean-pressure design} means: build the single steady flow of
the averaged \emph{state} --- the phase whose chamber pressure is
$\bar P_0$ --- and optimize that one flow. For the fixed bell this
is legitimate, because the bell's flow and thrust are \emph{linear}
in the phase pressure: the scaling law (Lemma~A) says the whole
field scales with $P_0$ on a fixed geometry, with the ambient
entering only additively through the pressure gauge. For a linear
problem the average of the solutions is the solution of the
average --- average-then-solve equals solve-then-average. That is
exactly why the bell collapses.

\emph{The averaged corner condition} instead solves each phase as
its \emph{own nonlinear flow}, lets each cast its own vote
$p_w(l^{*};\xi) - p_a$ at the truncation plane --- positive means
that phase is still pushing there and wants more plug, negative
means it is being dragged and wants less --- and averages the
\emph{optimality condition}:
$\int (p_w(l^{*};\xi) - p_a)\,\mathrm{d}\mu = 0$. The two
coincide only if the map from phase to vote is affine --- and the
plug breaks that affinity through its free edge: the ambient is
\emph{shared and fixed}, it does not scale with the phase, so each
phase's wall pressure saturates toward the \emph{same} $p_a$ at its
\emph{own} adaptation point. Mean-of-votes is not
vote-at-the-mean. The suite below measures the difference
directly: the mean-pressure design gives $l = 1.280$~m, the
averaged condition puts the optimum at $l^{*} = 2.103$~m ---
nowhere near. In one line: the bell result averages the
\emph{problem}; the plug result averages the \emph{condition}, and
averaging commutes with linear problems only.

\subsection{One march per phase covers every truncation}

In supersonic flow, disturbances cannot travel upstream: what
happens at a point is set entirely by a bounded region behind it,
its \emph{domain of dependence}. On a \emph{fixed} spike,
truncating at $l$ removes wall only downstream of $l$, so nothing
at $x < l$ can feel the cut. One full-spike march per phase
therefore yields the whole family of truncations in closed form,
\begin{equation}
J(l;\xi) \;=\; F_{\rm in}(\xi)
\;+\; \int_{x_0}^{l} \left(p_w(x;\xi) - p_a\right)
\,2\pi y_w\,(-\mathrm{d}y_w),
\label{eq:trunc}
\end{equation}
where every pressure is measured relative to ambient, so the free
edge contributes zero (the pressure on it \emph{is} $p_a$) and a
base plane at $p_b = p_a$ contributes zero as well. Differentiating
in $l$ gives the marginal condition $\mathrm{d}J/\mathrm{d}l = 0$,
i.e.\ $p_w(l) = p_a$ --- the plug corner condition at the
truncation plane: extend the plug exactly as far as its wall is
still pushing harder than the atmosphere. Its cycle average over
phases is the averaged corner condition above. The five-phase
truncation study therefore costs five marches, and every check
below reads the cached wall-pressure curves.

\subsection{The world}

Axisymmetric, real-gas NASA tables, one fixed piece of hardware
marched by every phase. Cowl lip at $(0, 2)$; annular flow arrives
at $M_i = 1.2$ aimed toward the axis ($\theta_i = -25^{\circ}$);
the lip fan --- the corner expansion of Chapter~\ref{ch:plug},
built per phase and consistent with the tables --- expands it down
to the shared ambient. The ambient is posed so that the fan turn is
about $|\theta_i|$, i.e.\ the flow leaves roughly axially
($\theta_E \approx 0$): the classic plug design condition, which
makes the spike --- one streamline of the mean phase's fan field
--- genuinely \emph{descending} toward the axis. The start line
poses the same $(M,\theta)(y)$ profile for every phase with each
phase's own thermodynamics: exact scaling-law data for the
pressure-only cycle, and the natural ``same injector Mach profile''
posing for the mock cycle. Each phase runs through the certified
plug march of Chapter~\ref{ch:plug} (prescribed wall, multi-column
foot search, row bookkeeping); the cycle itself is the certified
construction of Chapter~\ref{ch:cycle} (five log-uniform phases,
pressure ratio $PR = 10$, mock temperature ratio $TR = 1.306$).

\section{What was built}

\carrier{validation/a1\_plug\_cycle.py}: the world builder
(per-phase tables-consistent fan, mean-field spike streamline), the
per-phase march kits with disk cache, the closed-form truncation
family~\eqref{eq:trunc}, and two stages --- \code{t3} (pressure-only
cycle: the wall-level scaling law and the truncation-level
anti-collapse) and \code{mock} ($P$ and $T$ profiles: the ordering
of the per-phase optima, the measured adaptation break, the
averaged corner votes, the strict $\int\max > \max\int$ gap, the
peak-design penalty) --- plus a corrupted-ambient rejector.

\section{Results of record}

\textbf{Pressure-only cycle (the plug anti-collapse): 5/5 PASS.}
All phase marches Newton-certified. The wall-level scaling law
appears live exactly where it must and only there: on the part of
the wall still shielded from the ambient --- upstream of the first
edge-arrival, $x < 0.81$, found by tracing the edge's $C^{-}$ back
to the wall --- the scaled wall pressures $p_w/P_0$ collapse to one
curve to $4.7\times10^{-15}$, machine zero. Beyond that station the
collapse breaks, and that break \emph{is} the adaptation mechanism,
cleanly localized. The truncations then measure the dichotomy: the
cycle-optimal $l^{*} = 2.103$ sits far \emph{above} the
mean-pressure design ($l = 1.280$) and just below the peak design
($l = 2.336$) --- the plug does \emph{not} average, it shifts
toward the peak, held short of it only by the real adaptation
break. The per-phase optima
$l(\xi) = 2.336, 2.061, 1.125, 1.047, 1.006$ increase with the
phase pressure: the nesting \emph{order} of the plug theorem,
live.

\textbf{Mock RDE ($P$ and $T$ profiles), the genuine average: 9/9
PASS.} The same ordering appears again ($l(\xi) = 2.336 \ldots
1.012$). The non-ideal adaptation is \emph{measured}: each interior
phase loses thrust past its own $l(\xi)$ (relative losses
$1.6\times10^{-4}$ to $2.0\times10^{-3}$) --- the ideal clamp does
not hold in the real flow, and that loss is exactly the quantity
that breaks the ideal nesting. Consequently the strict inequality
of the sharpness caveat is resolved:
$\int\max - \max\int = 1.07\times10^{4}$ N ($2.3\times10^{-4}$ of
$J$), beyond its derived band; the penalty for designing at the
peak is $2.2\times10^{-4}$ of $J$, also resolved. At the blind
cycle optimum the averaged corner condition holds: the vote mean is
$+1.8\times10^{4}$ Pa against a balance band of $3.1\times10^{5}$,
while individual votes reach $\pm1.4\times10^{6}$ --- and the
optimum is a strict interior compromise (the per-phase optima
spread over 1.32 in $l$; no extreme phase owns $l^{*}$). A
corrupted ambient moves the vote mean $2.3\times$ outside the band
--- rejected. The road here --- two posing lessons and two checks
the theory itself rejected --- is Appendix~\ref{app:plugcycleposing}.

\textbf{A measured lesson that is new physics, not a bug.} The
bell's pointwise vote structure --- monotone in phase, exactly one
sign change (Chapter~\ref{ch:cycle}) --- does \emph{not} carry over
to the plug. Downstream of adaptation the wall pressure
\emph{oscillates} around $p_a$, because the lip fan reflects off
the free edge and comes back down onto the wall. Pointwise votes
therefore sample an oscillation, and the wave-passage phase can
vote negative at a length below its own optimum. Only the averaged
balance and the compromise structure survive --- which is exactly,
and only, what the sharpness caveat claims.
