\chapter{How this program decides that something is true}
\label{ch:method}

A design code is only as good as the evidence that it is right.
This chapter states the two rules every test suite in this document
obeys, explains why they were chosen, and defines the handful of
terms used to report results. It closes with the symbol table for
the whole document.

\section{Rule one: no tolerance is chosen by hand}

Every check in this document compares a computed number against a
band, and \emph{no band is picked by a human}. The reason is
simple: a tolerance chosen after seeing the answer certifies
nothing. It always exists, and it always passes.

Instead, the band is \emph{derived}, and in almost every case the
computation derives it about itself. The same quantity is computed
twice, once on a coarse discretization and once on a fine one. The
difference between the two is a measurement of the error the scheme
is still making. The band is that difference, multiplied by a fixed
safety factor $K_{\rm RICH} = 4$, plus a floor at the level of
floating-point roundoff:
\begin{equation}
\text{band} \;=\; K_{\rm RICH}\,
\bigl|\,\text{coarse} - \text{fine}\,\bigr|
\;+\; \text{roundoff floor}.
\label{eq:band}
\end{equation}
This is the classical idea of \emph{Richardson extrapolation} used
as an error estimate rather than as an accelerator, and the
resulting number is called a \emph{Richardson band} throughout
(Figure~\ref{fig:method}b). Because the method is second order
(Chapter~\ref{ch:moc}), halving the step should cut the error by
about four; when a suite reports a ``coarse/fine ratio of 4.52'' it
is reporting that the scheme is converging at the rate it should,
which is itself evidence that the band means something.

Not every quantity has a discretization to refine. Where it does
not, the band is derived from something else measurable --- the
scatter of the reference data itself, or the physical scale of the
quantity --- and the source is always stated. One chapter
(Chapter~\ref{ch:genotwin}) uses \emph{declared} thresholds rather
than derived ones; it says so explicitly, gives their provenance,
and explains why refinement would be the wrong ruler there. That
exception is deliberate and visible, which is the point.

\section{Rule two: every suite must be able to say no}

A test that has never failed may be measuring nothing. So alongside
the positive checks, each suite contains deliberate corruptions ---
called \emph{rejectors} --- which the suite is required to catch: a
sign flipped, a term dropped, a wrong ambient pressure, a wrong
characteristic foot, a geometry perturbed by a fraction of a
percent. If a rejector does \emph{not} fail, the corresponding
positive check is worthless and the step does not count as done.

Rejectors do real work in this document. They are what makes a
declared threshold trustworthy (they show the instrument resolves
defects an order of magnitude above the gap being measured), and
several of them caught genuine errors in the program before they
could reach a result.

Figure~\ref{fig:method}a puts both rules on one picture: every
check in this document, expressed as its measured deviation divided
by its own band, together with every deliberate corruption. The
positive checks must land left of one; the rejectors must land
right of it. Nothing is close to the line by accident.

(a)~Every certified check (blue) and every deliberate corruption

\section{How results are reported}

A few conventions recur.

\emph{``$m/n$ PASS''} counts the checks in a suite, rejectors
included: a suite of six positive checks and two corruptions that
behaves correctly reports 8/8.

\emph{``Newton-certified''} means every cell's converged residual
was verified to sit at roundoff level, scaled to the size of its
unknowns (Chapter~\ref{ch:moc}). The number quoted (``worst
residual 0.019'') is a fraction of the allowed tolerance, so
anything below one passes.

\emph{``Result of record''} means the number is quoted verbatim
from an actual run of the named carrier script, not recomputed for
the document. Chapter~\ref{ch:status} lists the one command that
reproduces each.

\emph{An oracle} is a case whose answer is known independently of
the program --- a closed-form solution, an exact conservation
identity, or a second code --- against which the program can be
judged. Where no oracle exists, a suite falls back on
\emph{internal closures}: conservation statements (mass, momentum)
that must hold whatever the answer is, plus \emph{alive controls},
which check that a term the program claims to include actually
changes the answer when removed.

Finally, and deliberately: \textbf{in this program the bugs the
tests caught are results too.} The main chapters describe each step
as it now works; every trap, wrong turn, and measured structural
lesson is collected in Appendix~\ref{ch:appendix}, cross-referenced
from the step it belongs to. A caught bug is evidence that the
instrument has teeth.
